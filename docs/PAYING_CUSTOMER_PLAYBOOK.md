# MatchSieve Paying-Customer Playbook

**Version:** 1.0  
**Status:** Strategy and copy hypotheses for validation — not evidence of proven conversion.  
**Primary launch hypothesis:** adult, English-speaking UK football researchers, initially focused on the Premier League.  
**Related documents:** [Customer personas](CUSTOMER_PERSONAS.md) · [Copy bank](COPY_BANK.md) · [Conversion experiments](CONVERSION_EXPERIMENTS.md)

## The commercial problem we are solving

Football scores, tables, form and many statistics are already available free. MatchSieve cannot credibly charge just for putting a score or a W/D/L strip on a screen. The paid value proposition is:

> **MatchSieve organises the available football evidence around the fixture and market question a person chooses, then makes the supporting signals, counter-evidence, source trail and gaps easy to understand.**

The product should help a customer do useful research with less repeated searching. It must not imply that research eliminates uncertainty or produces profit.

## The buyer's real job

The user does not wake up wanting a database, more charts or an AI score. They have a job to finish:

- **Persona A — independent researcher:** “Help me get from a fixture to a grounded view of one market without opening and reconciling a stack of pages.”
- **Persona B — critical comparator:** “Help me check whether an argument is actually supported, including the parts other people leave out.”
- **Persona C — publisher:** “Help me research several fixtures repeatably, retain traceable sources and publish my own useful explanation on time.”

Each landing page and campaign should lead with one job. Do not combine all three audiences into one overloaded headline. The generic product homepage can introduce the shared mechanism, then route users to the use case most like their own.

## Ten high-conversion principles, applied responsibly

1. **One audience, one problem, one next step.** A page should not ask a visitor to explore seven features before they understand the core job. Start with the fixture-and-market research problem; make “Explore the sample report” or “Research a match” the primary action, depending on the page.
2. **Specific beats grandiose.** Explain the mechanism—supporting signals, counter-evidence, source notes and missing inputs—instead of vague “AI-powered predictions” language.
3. **Demonstrate before asking for trust.** Put a real, reviewed sample report near the main claim. Label its fixture date, data sources, sample sizes and any limitations. Never use synthetic or fictional records as if they describe a real match.
4. **Make the value inspectable.** The best proof is a report the visitor can interrogate. Show where statements came from and how limited evidence is labelled. Use testimonials or customer numbers only after they exist, with permission and records.
5. **Reduce mental effort, not informed choice.** Keep the journey short: choose fixture → choose market → review brief. Explain specialist terms in plain English. Keep prices, report limits, renewals and cancellation terms visible.
6. **Acknowledge the free alternative.** Do not pretend that scores and basic stats are scarce. Explain the paid difference honestly: selection, market relevance, counter-evidence, source trail and a reusable report.
7. **Let users try the work, not merely read a slogan.** A sample should reveal the actual report structure. A visitor who understands the output is better qualified than a visitor who only clicked a dramatic headline.
8. **Earn repeat usage with a recurring job.** A weekly fixture slate, saved report library and repeatable research process may create a habit. Do not create a forced daily pick quota or notifications that pressure users to act.
9. **Use proof and deadlines only when real.** A deadline may refer to a genuine offer or upcoming fixture slate. Do not use resetting timers, fabricated user counts, false “last chance” claims, invented win records or false scarcity.
10. **Protect autonomy after purchase.** Make the report limits understandable and cancellation straightforward. Measure successful use and renewal, not just checkout conversion. Do not hide recurring billing or make the free option intentionally confusing.

These are product principles and hypotheses, not guaranteed conversion hacks. The only reliable way to learn which message converts our intended customer is to show it to that audience and measure real behaviour.

## Psychographic map

### What the three audiences value

| Dimension | A — Serious Independent Researcher | B — Critical Comparator | C — Football Publisher |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core motive | Preparedness and time saved | Independence and intellectual honesty | Credibility and reliable output under deadline |
| Wants to feel | Informed, organised, not rushed | Hard to mislead | Professional and well prepared |
| Dislikes | Tab-juggling and contradictory data | Unsupported certainty and selective records | Repeating research and publishing stale facts |
| Trust proof | Source timestamps, sample sizes, gaps | Counter-evidence, method and an honest record | Traceable sources, repeatable format and export |
| Strongest buying moment to test | Researching several fixtures in one sitting | A claim conflicts with the underlying record | Preparing a preview, show or community post |
| Repeat-use reason | Regular match-week workflow | Regularly evaluates different markets | Frequent publishing deadlines |
| Main uncertainty | “Is the output better than doing it myself?” | “Is this another disguised tipster?” | “Will it fit my workflow and save enough time?” |


- **Emotional trigger to validate:** “I can inspect the evidence, the case against it and the missing pieces. I can make my own judgement rather than rely on a stranger's confidence.”
- **Payment trigger to validate:** a serious adult researcher has a real fixture/market question, sees a real reviewed report that visibly replaces repeated cross-checking, understands the coverage/allowance and buys the £19/month UK pilot; second-report use and renewal are stronger evidence than compliments or clicks.
- **Positioning qualification:** “This is not for everyone. MatchSieve is for serious-minded adults who do their own research and value a disciplined process—not a tipster club, a sure-win service or a shortcut to profit.”

### Buying triggers: distinguish attention from willingness to pay

A matchday, a big fixture or a social-media post may create attention; it does not prove willingness to pay. Stronger signals are behaviours with a cost:

- The person supplies a real fixture and market to test.
- They inspect evidence and source notes rather than only the headline.
- They request another report or return in a later week.
- A publisher tries to use the report in their existing workflow.
- They complete a clearly described paid pilot and later renew.

Ask about the last real research session rather than asking “Would you pay for this?” Record the current workaround, how often the job occurs, consequences of doing it poorly, who else benefits, what the person currently pays for, and the action taken after viewing a real sample.

## Recommended funnel: value first, charge for repeatable value

### Stage 1 — Discovery
**Visitor question:** “What is this, and is it for someone like me?”  
**Message:** “One match. One market question. The evidence in one place.”  
**Proof:** short product explanation and a representative sample report.  
**Measure:** qualified sample opens and research starts, not raw impressions alone.

### Stage 2 — Consideration
**Visitor question:** “What do I get that a free score site does not?”  
**Message:** raw stats are ingredients; MatchSieve arranges available evidence for the selected market and shows what challenges the case and what is missing.  
**Proof:** a report with source details, sample sizes and visible limitations.  
**Measure:** sample report depth, report-to-research-start rate and users correctly describing the value after viewing it.

### Stage 3 — First useful result (activation)
**User job:** complete one research brief and understand it.  
**Product:** fixture search, market selection, clear loading/error states, readable report and a clear LIMITED_EVIDENCE state where needed.  
**Measure:** report generation completion, time to first report, source inspection, report save/export and user comprehension.

### Stage 4 — Paid conversion
**Offer:** charge for repeated useful research, with a clearly stated monthly allowance or fair-use rule.  
**Proof:** the user has seen the actual output; the plan limits and renewal terms are visible.  
**Measure:** paid conversion after a meaningful sample, payment failures, refunds and support questions. Do not launch paid checkout until billing and entitlement enforcement work.

### Stage 5 — Retention
**User question:** “Was this worth returning for?”  
**Product:** report history, reproducible snapshots, deliberate refresh, stable source attribution and clear notices when data is old or incomplete.  
**Measure:** repeat reports in a later week, paid renewal, cancellations and their reasons, and contribution margin by cohort.

### Stage 6 — Referral and publisher distribution
Ask satisfied customers to share a sample or refer someone only after they have experienced real value. For publishers, test cited export/reusable format and multiple-fixture workflow before promising a creator tier. Track qualified referrals and retained paying users, not just shares.

## Offer design and pricing guardrails

- Current UK price hypothesis: one Research Desk membership at £19/month with a proposed cap of 20 full research briefs per billing month. It is not validated and not live. The previous £7/£8/£10 ladder, cheap credit packs and matchday pass are retired from launch.
- Do not call a subscription “unlimited” until API/data costs and rate limits are measured.
- State exactly what a paid plan includes: report allowance, supported competitions, data freshness limits, saved history, export availability and what happens after the allowance is reached.
- Never show a reference price, discount, countdown or member count unless it is true and evidenced.
- A free sample should demonstrate quality without quietly starting a paid plan.
- Do not build acquisition around people who want to recover losses, need gambling to solve financial problems or are looking for guaranteed outcomes. Do not target minors.

## Measurement before persuasion gets stronger

The north-star is **paying customers who complete useful research and return**, with positive contribution margin. A high checkout rate paired with confusion, refunds or rapid cancellation is not success.

Instrument the funnel only after the product and privacy design support it. Prefer aggregated event counts and consented, minimal data. Do not log payment credentials, API keys, private free-text notes or more personal data than necessary.

## UK marketing and consumer-protection notes

MatchSieve is a research product adjacent to betting markets. Copy should be reviewed for the actual service and targeting before paid promotion. The UK's ASA/CAP guidance for tipsters warns against implying guaranteed success, unsupported winnings or typical profitable outcomes and says marketing should not play on financial fears. The ASA's gambling topic page reported an October 2026 ruling against an AI football analytics advertisement that implied users would be very likely to profit from gambling decisions. Avoid wording that suggests using analytics makes profit likely.

Official references:
- ASA/CAP, “Betting and gaming: Tipsters”: https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/betting-and-gaming-tipsters.html
- ASA, gambling topic and recent rulings: https://www.asa.org.uk/topic/Gambling.html
- ASA/CAP, “The odds are… it’s an ad!”: https://www.asa.org.uk/news/the-odds-are-its-an-ad-gambling-content-marketing-and-the-code.html
- US FTC, subscription dark-pattern warning: https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2021/10/ftc-ramp-enforcement-against-illegal-dark-patterns-trick-or-trap-consumers-subscriptions

These are source notes, not legal advice. Obtain relevant review before launch in each country.

## Decision rule

Do not scale traffic until we can answer yes to all of the following:

- Can a target user explain the paid difference from free score/stat sites after seeing a sample?
- Can users inspect the facts behind the report and see where evidence is weak?
- Do users complete the first research journey without help?
- Do real customers pay and return, not merely tell us the idea sounds good?
- Do repeat usage, data rights, support costs and payment fees leave positive contribution margin?
